# LORE.md — The World Bible (Season 1)

> **Status: living document — active brainstorm.** This is the canonical record of
> worldbuilding decisions as they are made. It is edited continuously during design
> sessions; anything marked *open* is undecided. Method and storage plan live in
> [WORLDBUILDING.md](WORLDBUILDING.md); the parked traversal design in
> [TRAVERSAL.md](TRAVERSAL.md). Spoiler warning for the entire game.

## Foundations

- **Grounding:** the world's mysticism is real esoteric tradition — alchemy,
  hermeticism, gnosticism, medieval folklore. No invented magic system. Chosen
  deliberately: the LLM already knows this material natively, so generated NPCs
  and lore sound correct without a lore-bible in every prompt.
- **Parallel Europe.** The world is modeled on old Europe without being it:
  geography and cultures rhyme with the real thing, and place names are drawn
  from real (often obscure) historical toponyms and naming morphology
  (-ford, -wick, -stead, -bury; -burg, -au further east). Generation inherits
  real folklore, trade-route logic, and regional texture for free. Authored
  names already chosen (Amberfall, Oakrun) are grandfathered; future naming
  follows the parallel-Europe route.
- **Central theme:** *the world is changing faster than its people can understand it.*
- **Tone:** begins as mystery, not apocalypse. Game-of-Thrones opening (life goes
  on, rumors at the edges), Dark-Souls delivery (truth through exploration, not
  exposition).
- **Player emotions per journey:** exploration, survival, mystery, tension.

## The truth (Season 1 canon)

The chain, from cause outward:

1. **Kingdom A** — ruled by a tiny elite (~0.1%) — pursued the Great Work
   generations ago and **released something**. The release is old; the current
   rulers are inheritors of the cover-up, guarding a secret they only partially
   understand.
2. **The released thing is the One** — the hermetic One Thing, the anima mundi.
   Not inherently evil: it is a mirror that takes the aspect of how it is used.
   Used selfishly by the elite, its expressed aspect is corruption. It obeys the
   unbalanced equation — alchemy is exchange, and this is taking-without-giving
   unleashed. It cannot be destroyed, only *paid* or *rebalanced*. It grows by
   taking (Hollow-Bastion-style seep from a hidden source). Its nature is
   ambiguous in-world and stays ambiguous in canon: it behaves like a process,
   but patterns suggest intent. Nobody knows. **There is a way to fix it** —
   the mystery has a true solution, however long it takes to find.
3. **The cover-up is arithmetic, not sadism.** Kingdom A's elite experiment on
   humans to balance the ledger with other people's lives — cheaper currency.
   Disappeared villages are installments.
4. **The experiments produce homunculi** (see grammar below). Some escape.
5. **The evidence points at Kingdom B** — partly engineered by A, partly accident
   of geography. B's leadership is genuinely ignorant, panics, and **bans
   alchemy** — criminalizing the only discipline that could identify what a
   homunculus is. The lie defends itself.
6. **Kingdom B blames alchemy; fringe alchemists hold the real clues** and are
   hunted for it.
7. **Players start in Kingdom F — named Amberfall** — unrelated, distant. The
   mystery arrives as news, refugees, and rumor. Investigating is optional;
   the sandbox is the point. Amberfall's capital is the first big city
   players typically visit; the starting town is a waystation on its road.

### The Order of the Red King

- **The outer face is Kingdom A's ordinary state religion** — unremarkable on
  the surface. Within it hides an inner sect: **the Order of the Red King**,
  keepers of the true history, seated in the court (A is monarchy-centered;
  the conspiracy is a crown conspiracy, not a church one).
- **There is an actual Red King.** The founding king of A began the Great
  Work generations ago and released the One. Official history says he died
  centuries ago. The truth: the immortality half-worked, and something that
  used to be him persists — hidden, diminished, sustained by the
  installments. The Order is named after its master, not a symbol.
- **The double meaning is armor:** alchemy-literate players will read "Red
  King" as the emblem of the completed stone (rubedo) — scholarship points
  the right way, but the truth is worse than the metaphor.
- The experiments, the payments, the schedule — all of it is done *on his
  behalf*. What the Order ultimately intends (restore him? complete him?
  replace him?) is *open*, and is a candidate for the season's final
  question.

### Information travels slowly; the elites don't

- **Slow information is a world rule.** Couriers, carriages, and rural
  word-of-mouth: news moves at travel speed, rumors distort with distance,
  and players themselves become carriers of knowledge between towns.
- **The elites secretly know how to create portals** (among other arts).
  It is how a generations-old conspiracy stays ahead: they move faster than
  truth. They infiltrate high positions across kingdoms — including,
  potentially, F's own court.
- **Portals are physical and very sparse.** Deep in some dungeons (e.g. the
  field site near Town F), a player may find a fixed portal linking to
  another dungeon near a distant town. Finding one is a major discovery —
  both a shortcut and evidence of who built it.
- **The rot appears to come from many places** because the elites seed decoy
  outbreaks via teleportation. Naive triangulation of the rot's spread fails
  *by design* — the world's map has been tampered with.
- **The deeper clue is timeline forensics, not geometry.** Real rot grows
  continuously from its source; seeded fronts start abruptly, full-strength,
  on a date, with no prior symptoms in courier logs. Old letters, dated
  rumors, and travel records are evidence items. A clue built for a
  community: it takes shared observation across weeks to see.

### The lie is authored content

Players will spend their early game *inside* the cover story, very possibly
working for the elites' interests via innocent-looking quests (clear the ruins,
hunt the heretic, recover the relic — actually containment and evidence
destruction). The official story gets the same authoring care as the truth.

### The sleeper in Town F

- **Kingdom A keeps sleeper cells**: dormant agents, deeply integrated —
  families, respected roles — activated only by communication from home.
  Their purpose is not spreading rot but continuing experimentation and
  steering local leaders toward A's agenda. Normally cities only; towns are
  too small. This town is the exception.
- **Why this town: it is staging for a planned experiment.** The sleeper will
  be the cause of the town's consumption by rot, around the **one-month
  mark** of the season. (Reasoning behind the experiment itself: *open*.)
- **His name is Basil** — a herb name, maximally cozy, zero suspicion; his
  shop's shelves carry "Basil's Cures." Chosen over names with hidden
  meanings: his disguise is total.
- **His cover is the town apothecary.** He was not studying townsfolk from
  day one: after earlier experiments, homunculus sightings increased near
  this town, and A placed him to study them up close. His insider knowledge
  means his remedies against black rot and homunculi are *unusually
  effective* — the town's healer and best supplier is the enemy's agent.
- **The cure.** Players can bring homunculi to him: rarely, and at a steep
  price, he sells branded "cures" (labeled under his name) that turn a
  homunculus into an NPC who can join the party — recruitment through
  mercy. Materials are limited; it is never common.
  - His cures are *not* standard potions: he deliberately includes a
    memory-wiping component. The wipe is a feature, not a flaw — cured
    homunculi return with absent or fragmented memories, but fragments
    slip out (a muttered place-name from a cured party member is a clue
    channel).
  - His first batches didn't cure at all — the cure line is his own
    experimentation, iterated on paying customers.
  - **Much later**, an independent alchemist (one of the persecuted fringe)
    discovers a cure *without* the wiper — cured homunculi keep their
    memories. Witnesses unlock, and his additive is exposed as deliberate:
    why would a healer erase memories on purpose?
- **The economy is his bodyguard.** When first suspected or caught, he buys
  goodwill with genuinely excellent goods. Players who suspect him are
  reluctant to kill their best source of anti-rot remedies — saving the town
  costs the community its supplier. And every cure he sells doubles as a
  field trial: players fighting homunculi with his potions are running his
  experiment for him.
- **The trigger is human: duty over love.** His wife and children are real —
  a life genuinely built while dormant. The activation order arrives around
  the one-month mark; he obeys, dosing the wells with his own hands.
- **He escapes with his family** after the deed. On later runs (or deep
  exploration), there is a procgen chance he resurfaces with them in some
  distant town under another name — a recurring fugitive encounter injected
  through generation, not scripted.
- **Preventable with cross-run knowledge:** on later runs, players can
  discover that if he dies before the experiment, the town is not consumed —
  and because the trigger is his choice, there is a second route: he can be
  *turned* (through his family) rather than killed.
- **Author causes, simulate consequences.** What follows from players
  intervening (mourning, suspicion, Kingdom A noticing a missed check-in) is
  not hand-written content — it emerges from NPC context, world events, and
  rot rules. A itself is an actor with simple rules, not a script.
- **Subtlety by dormancy:** he is genuinely a good citizen — the tells are
  negative space (never speculates about the plague, never travels, no
  history before ~15 years ago) plus one buried anomaly in mundane records
  (a supply order with one reagent no honest trade needs). Dialogue must be
  written to survive re-reading after the unmasking.

### The parallel-Europe cultural map

- **Amberfall (F): English pastoral** — shires, hedgerows, warm inns; names
  in the -run/-ford/-wick register. Monarchy present but distant and benign
  from Oakrun's view.
- **Kingdom A: Bohemian crown** — Prague-flavored spired cities, Germanic
  names (-burg, -au). Crucially **monarchy-centered, not church-centered**:
  the conspiracy lives in the court. Real-history anchor: Rudolf II's
  Prague, where the crown itself patronized alchemists.
  **Drazna** is its distant lake-city and royal seat: the destination named
  by Oakrun's erased east road, and a concrete long-term goal for travelers
  such as Edda Marr and Wren.
- **Kingdom B: French Gothic church country** — cathedrals, piety, clerical
  authority. The alchemy ban came from the pulpit: a culture that already
  distrusted the art needed only a push. Persecuted alchemists hide in
  cathedral cities.
- Three silhouettes even in rumor form: shires, spires, cathedrals.

## The Magnum Opus spine

The elites effectively began the Great Work **on the world itself** — and
abandoned it at the first stage when it went wrong. The decay is not an attack;
it is a transmutation left half-finished.

- **The stages are the seasons.** *Nigredo* (blackening, dissolution) is
  Season 1. When the community finds the cure, they complete the stage
  correctly and the world advances to *albedo* — Season 2. Then *citrinitas*,
  then *rubedo*. The game's roadmap is the Magnum Opus.
- **The world cannot be restored, only carried through.** The fix is never
  "undo it" — it is completing the Work unselfishly.
- **The black rot** is nigredo made physical: a spreading corruption that
  consumes people, places, and things. It is the visible face of the decay
  and the doom clock's hand.
- **The rot began long ago** — but in a slow-information world it stayed a
  rumor for generations. At game start there is a **belief gradient by
  distance**: in Town F and the first kingdom it's tavern talk some scoff at
  (the White-Walker opening), further out it is visibly real, and at the
  frontier it is undeniable. Traveling outward is traveling up the truth.
- **The end state:** the rot's effects compound until it consumes the
  world's core — that is what world-death is.
- **No doom timer in the UI.** The screen shows time and day only; the
  community infers the deadline from the rot's advance. The clock is read
  from geography, not a countdown.
- **Rotted regions are transformed, not deleted.** A consumed region becomes a
  corrupted variant — same geometry, spawns swapped to rotted enemy variants
  and homunculi — still enterable and dangerous, until it eventually seals
  over once fully digested. Cheap to implement (spawn-table swap + region
  state), rich in play (studying the rot up close).
- The LLM knows these stages natively — ruins, rites, and heretic texts can
  reference nigredo/albedo/rubedo and stay coherent without prompting.

## The doom clock

- The world has a real-time lifespan (working figure: **~3 months**). If the
  community fails to solve the mystery in time, **the world dies**.
- **Failure is expected and is the arc:** the same Season 1 truth replays after
  each world-death. Knowledge persists in players' heads, not saves — an
  Outer-Wilds loop played by a community. Popularity and progress compound
  across attempts until the mystery is solved before the clock ends.
- What happens after a successful solve: **the world advances to the next stage
  of the Work** — albedo is Season 2 (see the Magnum Opus spine).
- Long-term (parked): reshuffled-conspiracy variants where the ledger becomes a
  template and generation re-deals the guilty party. Superseded for now by the
  stage roadmap; revisit only if stages run out or replayability demands it.

## Homunculi — a generation grammar

Homunculi are combinations, not a bestiary: `human + animal`, `human + monster`,
degradation stages, escape generations. A closed vocabulary of components yields
a combinatorial family — the same pattern as the loot system's pools. Also the
game's central physical evidence: each homunculus is a clue about what was done
and where.

## Town F: the waystation

The starting slice. Design intent: **homely** — players should feel this is
home, comfortable and worth saving, backed by the distant comfort of the
mother kingdom.

- **The town is named Oakrun** — the great oak at the crossroads where
  carriages change horses. Sturdy, plain, trustworthy: "Oakrun, on the
  Amberfall road."
- **A waystation town on the kingdom road** — it exists because carriages
  change horses here. The last comfortable stop before the wilds; orchards
  and small farms around it.
- **The inn is the town's heart** — fireplace, stew, gossip. In a
  slow-information world, a waystation is where news physically arrives:
  couriers and refugees pass through the common room. Rumors, the
  noticeboard, and overheard talk are the natural quest surface.
- **The carriage stop is built-in** — the transport prerequisite lands here
  naturally.
- **Nearby (leaning): Kingdom A's field site** — the semi-main area of the
  slice, a connector-chain away. It is why homunculus sightings rose, why
  Basil was posted here, and the first physical evidence players can reach.
